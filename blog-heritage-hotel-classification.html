What Makes a Hotel a "Heritage Hotel"?

5 February 2026 · The Diggi Journal

"Heritage hotel" gets used loosely across travel booking sites — sometimes meaning genuinely historic, sometimes meaning a modern building with a few antique-style lamps. In India, though, it's a specific, government-recognised classification, and understanding it helps travellers tell the difference.

The official classification

India's Ministry of Tourism classifies heritage hotels into categories — broadly, Heritage, Heritage Classic and Heritage Grand — based on the age, scale and condition of the property. To qualify at all, a hotel generally needs to be housed in a building constructed before a certain cut-off date (historically, pre-1950), retain its original architectural character, and demonstrate genuine historical or architectural significance rather than a themed reconstruction.

Why the distinction matters

A classified heritage hotel has usually been vetted for the things a marketing page can't fake: the age and authenticity of the structure, the preservation of original features like courtyards, jharokhas (overhanging enclosed balconies) and stonework, and often a documented family or royal history connected to the building. This is different from a newly built hotel decorated in a "heritage style" — both can be beautiful, but only one is actually old.

What to look for as a guest

If heritage authenticity matters to your trip, ask directly: is the property classified, how old is the structure, and does the family that built it still have any connection to it? Diggi Palace, for example, has been the ancestral residence of the Diggi royal family since 1860 and is classified as a heritage hotel by the Government of India — the palace continues to function partly as a family home alongside its hotel operations.

Beyond the classification

The classification is a useful signal, but the real test is what a stay feels like — whether the rooms carry genuine age and irregularity rather than uniform modern layout, whether the gardens and courtyards feel lived-in rather than landscaped for guests, and whether the staff can tell you real stories about the building rather than reciting a script.
